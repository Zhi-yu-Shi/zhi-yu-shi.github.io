I develop multiscale computational methods to understand and predict the behavior of chemically reacting systems and to guide their design for sustainable energy and green manufacturing. My work combines scientific machine learning, atomistic simulation, and multiphysics CFD to study how molecular reactions shape the performance of propulsion and catalytic systems. I am particularly interested in how we can steer reactions in gases and on surfaces by controlling when and where energy is deposited and how reactive species are transported.

## Research

For scientific research and engineering design, the most interesting thing happens when a nonmonotonic trend is found. That is where interesting physics and chemistry arise and when a strategy of designing and optimizing a complex engineering system emerge. In my research, I study how nonequilibrium plasma discharges nonmonotonically control combustion phenomena. In combustion/propulsion, ignition is the starting and core process. I study how nonequilibrium energy input from different plasma discharges changes when and where a fuel ignites and thus mononotonically control ignition performance. More complicated combustion phenomena, such as deflagration to detonation transition, also occurs with strong auto-ignition sequences during local explosion. I developed multi-dimensional simulation tools to investigate how nonequilibrium plasma discharges nonmonotonically control the DDT onset. In catalytic systems such as ammonia synthesis, the central question is, can plasma enhance ammonia-synthesis efficiency beyond the limitations of the traditional Haber-Bosch process? I study how a plasma changes the catalyst structure itself and thus initiate new surface reaction pathways. Collaborating with [Yiteng Zheng](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=v95Bo_cAAAAJ&hl=en), we found and explained why N2/H2 plasma nonmonotonically produce nickel nitride formation at different feed ratios and thus lead to nonmonotonic NH3 production via alternative Mars–van Krevelen pathway. AI can significantly accelerate all scales of simulations with its strong interpolation capabilities and, if trained well, good accuracy, efficiency, and generalization. For example, AI can fundamentally change how people study reactive events relevant to propulsion and energy conversion. Interatomic potentials learned from quantum calculations let us follow larger systems for longer times and sample the many molecular encounters needed to predict reaction rates. For hydrogen combustion, I developed a potential that combined two levels of quantum chemistry and reproduced benchmark rate constants for an elementary reaction with more than 80% reduced computational cost, collaborating with [Ahren Jasper](https://www.anl.gov/profile/ahren-w-jasper) and [Stephen Klippenstein](https://www.anl.gov/profile/stephen-j-klippenstein). With [Aditya Lele](https://engineering.rowan.edu/faculty_staff/listing/aditya_lele.html) and [Mark Martirez](https://www.pppl.gov/people/mark-martirez), we used a machine-learned force field to study how temperature modulation affects ammonia synthesis on iron. At the scale of reaction networks, I also co-developed Stiff-PINN working with [Sili Deng](https://meche.mit.edu/people/faculty/silideng@mit.edu), which uses the separation between fast and slow reactions to solve stiff chemical kinetics with physics-informed neural networks. ## Research direction

I aim to develop physics-informed surrogate models for multidisciplinary design optimization in aerospace propulsion and, more broadly, energy conversion. A central question is: which details of molecular chemistry must we resolve to make a reliable engineering design choice?

1. **Coupling electron kinetics to reacting flows.** I plan to develop an ML-accelerated solver for the electron Boltzmann equation that can run within a reacting-flow simulation. This would let me study how a changing gas mixture redistributes electron energy among excitation, chemical reactions, and heating, and how those changes feed back on the flow during transient propulsion operation.

2. **Predicting reaction pathways at catalytic interfaces.** I plan to develop machine-learned interatomic potentials to explore competing reaction pathways at gas–solid and liquid–solid interfaces as catalyst surfaces evolve under operating conditions. My goal is to identify which surface structures favor a desired product and use that knowledge to guide the choice of catalyst materials.

3. **Using design uncertainty to guide molecular simulation.** I plan to use surrogate models of coupled chemistry, flow, and heat transfer to compare candidate designs and identify the reaction rates whose uncertainty could change their ranking. I can then target those reactions with higher-fidelity atomistic calculations and bring the refined rates back into the design comparison.
